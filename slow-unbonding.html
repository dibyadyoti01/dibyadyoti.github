The Slow Unbonding

On Love, Loneliness, and What We Owe Each Other

Originally published on Substack · 2026

Midnight arrives the same way for everyone, and yet nobody survives it the same way. Someone lingers in a room full of people, terrified of the silence that waits outside it. Someone else scrolls, thumb moving faster than thought, chasing a feed that never quite fills the hour. Someone cries quietly, careful not to be heard. And someone else just clenches their jaw and tells themselves, just man up, dude — as if the ache were a weakness to be disciplined rather than a signal to be understood.

Different rituals, same hour, same hunger underneath: to be held by something larger than the self.

That hunger is what Émile Durkheim spent his life trying to explain. Reading him, one idea kept surfacing above the rest — that a society survives not by how much it produces or accumulates, but by how well it integrates. Norms, traditions, shared consensus, even the noise of political discourse — all of it, in Durkheim’s telling, exists to bind people together, to regulate the self against the pull of pure self-interest. Integration, not captivation. The point was never to dazzle each other. It was to hold each other.

Somewhere along the way, that purpose got inverted.

From Giving to Getting

The original design of the bond — help others, offer what you have, fill what’s lacking in someone else — has quietly turned inside out. Now the operating question isn’t what can I give, but what can I extract. Call it crony capitalism, call it something less namable — a slow cultural drift where every relationship starts to resemble a transaction, and every transaction is expected to yield a return.

You can see it most clearly in how an entire generation has learned to date. Scroll through enough reels and shorts and you’ll find the same joke told a hundred ways: love reduced to a spreadsheet. Looks, income, height, follower count, “standards” — categories in a checklist, each one a box to be ticked before a person is allowed to matter. It’s not really about love anymore. It’s about audit. What are you bringing to this, and how much can I take from it?

And because everyone is auditing everyone else, everyone is also performing for the audit — maintaining a version of themselves polished enough to pass inspection, glamorous enough to be chosen, safe enough to never be left. Dating, marriage, even friendship start to feel like a stage where the real fear isn’t what if it doesn’t work out but what if I’m found lacking. So people hold on to the performance instead of the person, and call the performance love because it’s easier to sustain.

Compare that to what the Bhagavad Gita says love actually is: an act of giving that expects nothing back. Unconditional, not because it’s naive, but because conditions turn love into a contract, and contracts can always be broken by non-payment. The modern version of love, though, seems to have flipped this on its head — it begins not with what can I offer but with whether the message has been read, whether the tick has turned blue, and if not, next person, please.

It’s worth sitting with how young this script gets written. A fourteen-year-old planning the theme of a wedding they haven’t been proposed to, while too arrogant to apologize to a friend they mocked for their parents’ divorce — that’s not an isolated absurdity. That’s the curriculum. We are teaching the aesthetics of commitment long before we teach the substance of care.

Performing Both Worlds at Once

There’s a strange duality running underneath all of this — an attempt to hold onto spirituality and materialism simultaneously, each performed convincingly enough that neither collapses the other. Like Schrödinger’s cat, both states exist until observed too closely, and most of us have gotten very good at not looking too closely at our own contradictions. We want the calm of unconditional love and the currency of conditional status, and we try to keep both alive at once, mostly out of ego, sometimes out of exhaustion.

The same inversion shows up in religion. It was, at its foundation, meant to be a container for peace — a structure built to guard against violence, to hold communities together the way Durkheim described. And yet now there are wars fought in its name, “saving” a religion through the very violence it was designed to prevent. The tool built for integration has, in places, become a weapon for division.

Where the Poison Starts

None of this begins in adulthood. It begins small — a mother quietly telling her son not to share his notebook with the neighbor’s daughter. It begins with a child taught to resent Ravi for scoring higher, rather than taught to sit with Ravi and understand how he did it. These are tiny moments, barely noticed, but they are the first drops of a poison that eventually pulls people away from bonding, away from love, away from the very integration that was supposed to hold them upright.

And it doesn’t stay small. It grows into a twenty-eight-year-old in Bangalore, earning well, technically thriving, lying awake at midnight and still hoping — quietly, almost embarrassed by the hope — that someday someone will actually see him. That someone will offer a shoulder without calculating what it costs them to lend it.

That hope isn’t naive. It’s the oldest human instinct there is — the one Durkheim wrote about, the one the Gita wrote about, the one that predates every checklist and every algorithm. We were built to bond. We still are. Somewhere underneath all the performing and auditing and quiet resentment, that original design is still intact, waiting to be used the way it was meant to be.

Maybe the work now isn’t to reinvent love or reinvent society. Maybe it’s just to unlearn the checklist — to go back to giving without immediately asking what it will return. Nothing quite as radical as revolution. Just a little less performance, a little less drama, and a little more willingness to hold the person in front of you without first checking if they’ve earned it.

The world could stand to be a little less dramatic. It could stand, too, to remember its own principles.
