The Architecture of Status: Capitalism, Democracy, White-Collar Prestige and the Fragility of the Middle Class

An unfinished inquiry into economic power, social hierarchy, professional identity and the human obsession with recognition.

Dibyadyoti Sarkar  ·  19 November 2026  ·  15–18 min read

I have been thinking about the strange relationship between occupation, money, recognition and human worth. Why do we instinctively treat certain professions as more respectable than others? Why does a government designation, a corporate title or a particular salary seem to change the way society looks at an individual? And why do so many of us, consciously or otherwise, participate in this ranking even when we claim to believe in equality?

Perhaps the answer is not simply that people admire success. It is that we have built a social vocabulary in which economic position is often mistaken for personal worth.

A person working in a glass office, carrying a laptop and receiving a monthly salary may be perceived as more successful than someone doing physically demanding work that keeps a city functioning. A senior official may receive deference that has little to do with their character. A professional qualification may become a social identity, and that identity may gradually turn into a claim of superiority.

But what exactly are we recognising: skill, contribution, income, scarcity of expertise, institutional authority, or merely the symbols associated with a particular class?

I do not think these things are interchangeable. Yet our social behaviour often treats them as if they were.

If two people contribute equally to the well-being of society but receive very different levels of income and recognition, what does that tell us about the values embedded in our economic system?

We did not invent hierarchy yesterday

Occupational hierarchy predates modern capitalism. In pre-industrial societies, status was frequently inherited through birth, land ownership, caste, religious authority, military position or proximity to political power. Work was not merely an economic activity; it was also a marker of one's place in a social order.

Industrialisation transformed this arrangement without eliminating hierarchy. It created new forms of wealth and new occupational categories: factory owners, managers, engineers, clerks, accountants, administrators and wage workers. Education and salaried employment became important routes to mobility. The modern professional class expanded, and credentials increasingly determined access to institutional positions.

Max Weber's distinction between class, status and party helps make sense of this. Class concerns one's economic position and life chances; status concerns social honour and prestige; party concerns organised power. These dimensions overlap, but they are not identical. A person may have social prestige without great wealth, wealth without cultural respectability, or formal authority without broad social admiration.

In India, these distinctions have particular historical weight. Caste-based occupational ordering, colonial administrative structures, access to English-language education and post-independence public institutions all shaped the social meaning of work. Modern education and government employment opened routes to mobility, but they also acquired symbolic value far beyond the salary attached to a job.

The civil servant, doctor, engineer, professor or corporate executive is not admired only for the work performed. The designation can signify security, family advancement, access to networks and escape from older forms of deprivation.

That aspiration is understandable. For many families, professional success represents a real transformation in material conditions and social opportunity. The question is not whether people should aspire to such work. The question is when a legitimate aspiration becomes a hierarchy of human value.

The white-collar promise

White-collar employment carries a particular promise: stable income, a respectable identity, indoor work, institutional recognition and a degree of control over one's future. It is often presented as the destination of education and upward mobility.

But the social prestige attached to white-collar work can conceal the economic dependence that comes with it. A salaried professional may have a good income and still depend almost entirely on the continuation of a job. Their lifestyle, housing loan, vehicle loan, school fees and family obligations may all be calibrated to a monthly salary.

This is not an argument that professional work is easy or that all occupations are equivalent in skill, training or responsibility. Doctors, engineers, researchers, teachers and administrators perform work that requires specialised knowledge and can carry substantial consequences. Differences in expertise and responsibility matter. But they do not establish differences in human dignity.

Pierre Bourdieu's work on cultural capital helps explain how prestige reproduces itself. Accent, language, educational institutions, taste, mannerisms and professional networks can function as signals of belonging. People learn not only how to acquire qualifications but also how to perform the identity associated with a particular class.

At what point does professional confidence become social distance? When does pride in one's work become contempt for someone else's?

There is also a subtler problem: when a designation becomes central to identity, criticism of the institution can feel like criticism of the self. The person may begin defending the prestige of the profession rather than examining the actual quality of the work.

Capitalism rewards contribution, but not contribution alone

Markets are powerful coordination mechanisms. Prices communicate information, competition can discipline inefficient producers, and the prospect of profit can encourage investment and innovation. It would be simplistic to dismiss these functions.

But market income is not a direct measurement of social usefulness. It reflects a combination of demand, scarcity, bargaining power, ownership, productivity, institutional rules and purchasing power. A highly valuable social activity may be poorly paid if the people who need it cannot pay much. A profitable activity may generate substantial returns because the owner controls a scarce asset or occupies a powerful market position.

Marx placed ownership of the means of production at the centre of his critique of capitalism. Whatever one makes of his broader conclusions, the distinction between income earned through labour and income derived from ownership remains essential. Two individuals may receive similar annual incomes while occupying very different positions: one depends on wages, the other owns assets that generate returns.

This distinction becomes clearer when we separate income from wealth. Income is a flow over a period of time. Wealth is a stock of assets, net of liabilities, accumulated across time. Wealth can provide security, collateral, inheritance and influence. It can also generate further wealth.

Research by the World Inequality Lab, including the work of Nitin Kumar Bharti, Lucas Chancel, Thomas Piketty and Anmol Somanchi, estimated that India's top 1% received 23.3% of national income and held 40.1% of wealth in 2022–23. These estimates are not a complete description of every household's circumstances, and they depend on the methodology used. But they underline an important point: the distribution of economic assets is highly unequal.

So when we celebrate high salaries as the ultimate sign of success, are we overlooking the more consequential question of who owns the assets that generate income even when their owners are not working?

If a society rewards ownership more reliably than socially necessary labour, should we describe its distribution of rewards as a pure meritocracy?

The middle-class trap: respectable, but not necessarily secure

The middle class occupies an interesting position in the hierarchy. It may enjoy education, consumer choice, urban amenities and a degree of social respectability. Yet a substantial part of its financial security can depend on uninterrupted employment.

A household can appear prosperous while carrying significant fixed obligations: rent or mortgage payments, education expenses, healthcare costs, vehicle repayments, insurance premiums and support for relatives. The visible signs of consumption do not necessarily reveal the balance sheet underneath.

This is where the distinction between income and financial resilience matters. A high monthly salary does not automatically imply the ability to withstand unemployment, illness, a market downturn or a major family emergency. Savings, liquid assets, debt exposure, insurance and access to public services all influence resilience.

Thorstein Veblen's idea of conspicuous consumption helps explain why consumption can become a social performance. Goods are purchased not only for their utility but also for the status they communicate. Fred Hirsch's concept of positional goods adds another layer: some advantages depend on one's relative position. A prestigious school, a scarce credential or a desirable neighbourhood cannot be made universally available in the same way as an ordinary mass-produced good.

This produces a competition that can intensify even when everyone's material standard of living improves. If status depends on being ahead of others, then collective progress does not necessarily reduce status anxiety.

Why do we sometimes feel unsuccessful despite having more material comfort than previous generations? How much of that dissatisfaction is absolute deprivation, and how much is the experience of comparison?

There is no single middle-class experience. Some households have substantial assets and security; others are one income shock away from serious hardship. The useful question is not whether the middle class is secretly poor. It is whether social respectability is being mistaken for economic independence.

Political equality does not automatically mean economic equality

Democracy establishes a powerful principle: citizens possess equal political status, at least in the formal act of voting. But equal votes do not necessarily produce equal influence over public decisions.

Economic resources can shape political access through campaign finance, lobbying, media ownership, organised interest groups, policy expertise and the ability to sustain long-term advocacy. This does not mean that every wealthy person controls government or that democratic institutions are meaningless. It means that political equality requires more than the existence of elections.

Antonio Gramsci's concept of hegemony offers a way to think about how power operates through ideas and institutions as well as coercion. A social order becomes durable when its assumptions begin to appear like common sense. People may come to accept a hierarchy not because someone explicitly commands them to do so, but because its values are repeated through education, media, family expectations and everyday interactions.

Could the belief that some professions are inherently more respectable be one such assumption? Could the idea that market income is a reliable measure of merit make unequal outcomes appear more deserved than they really are?

India's electoral bonds episode also illustrates why transparency in the relationship between money and politics matters. In February 2024, the Supreme Court struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme, holding that the non-disclosure of political funding information violated the right to information under Article 19(1)(a). The judgment did not settle every question about political finance, but it reaffirmed that citizens need meaningful information to assess the relationship between political parties and financial contributors.

Democracy therefore faces a continuing challenge: how can political institutions remain responsive to citizens when economic resources are distributed so unevenly?

Employment, education and the promise of merit

We often describe education as the great equaliser. It can be one. Literacy, public schooling, universities, scholarships and professional training can expand opportunities that would otherwise remain inaccessible.

But education also operates as a system of selection. Credentials signal ability, persistence and specialised training, yet they can also reflect unequal access to coaching, language, networks, institutional reputation and family resources. Michael Spence's work on job-market signalling helps explain why qualifications can function as signals to employers even when the credential itself does not fully measure productive ability.

As more people acquire degrees, employers may raise credential requirements for jobs that have not fundamentally changed. This is sometimes described as credential inflation. The result can be an expensive competition for positional advantage rather than a proportionate increase in productive skill.

India's labour market also needs to be examined beyond a single unemployment rate. The Periodic Labour Force Survey's usual-status unemployment rate for people aged 15 and above was 3.2% in 2023–24. That headline figure is useful, but it does not by itself describe underemployment, job security, wages, informality, working conditions or the availability of suitable work. A person may be counted as employed while still experiencing unstable or inadequate work.

Why do families often value a secure government job so intensely? It is not merely a matter of prestige. Predictable income, benefits, social protection and protection from some forms of market volatility are rational considerations. The status attached to public employment may partly reflect the scarcity of secure jobs elsewhere.

But if security is the real attraction, perhaps the broader policy question should be how to make decent work and social protection less dependent on a narrow set of occupations.

Artificial intelligence and the instability of professional status

Artificial intelligence introduces a new uncertainty into this hierarchy. Many occupations that once appeared protected by specialised knowledge now contain tasks that machines can perform, assist or accelerate.

This does not mean that entire professions will disappear at once. The International Labour Organization's 2025 global index of generative AI exposure emphasises that transformation of tasks is generally more likely than complete automation of occupations. Exposure is not the same as a forecast of job losses. The effects will vary by task, sector, institution, regulation and the way organisations choose to deploy the technology.

Still, the psychological implications are significant. If a degree, professional title or specialised skill once guaranteed a certain degree of scarcity, what happens when the relevant knowledge becomes easier to access or reproduce?

Could the prestige of some white-collar occupations decline even while their economic importance remains substantial? Could new hierarchies emerge around ownership of AI systems, data, computing infrastructure, intellectual property and access to capital?

Technological change does not automatically democratise opportunity. The gains from productivity depend on ownership, bargaining power, competition policy, taxation, education and social protection. A more productive economy can still be an unequal one.

Perhaps the more important question is not whether AI will replace professionals. It is whether the benefits of higher productivity will be broadly shared, or concentrated among those who own and control the systems that make it possible.

The psychology of acquired ego

Social comparison is a normal feature of human psychology. Leon Festinger's social comparison theory proposed that people evaluate themselves partly by comparing their abilities and opinions with those of others. Such comparisons can help us learn, set goals and understand where we stand.

But comparison can also turn into a permanent status contest. A person's satisfaction becomes dependent on being ahead of someone else. The colleague's promotion becomes a personal defeat. Another person's salary becomes evidence of one's own inadequacy. A job title becomes something to defend because it has become fused with self-esteem.

There is a difference between taking pride in achievement and treating achievement as proof of superiority. The first can motivate excellence. The second can produce contempt, insecurity and a need for constant validation.

And this is not a problem confined to wealthy or powerful people. Status anxiety can exist at every income level. People may look down on one group while feeling inferior to another. The hierarchy reproduces itself because almost everyone can locate someone below them and someone above them.

What would happen if we stopped using occupation as a shortcut for judging character? Would we become less ambitious, or would ambition become less dependent on comparison?

The contradiction I cannot simply stand outside

I should acknowledge my own position in this discussion. I am not writing as someone who has escaped the attraction of professional status. I have ambitions. I value education, institutional responsibility and the possibility of meaningful public work. I understand why families want their children to enter secure and respected professions.

I also recognise that administrative authority can be used to improve public services, implement policy and address structural problems. It would be absurd to argue that all designations are meaningless or that expertise does not matter.

But I want to distinguish between the value of doing consequential work and the social prestige that accumulates around the title of the person doing it.

If I aspire to public service, I would like the aspiration to be grounded in the work itself: the quality of institutions, the ability to solve problems, the responsibility to citizens and the possibility of reducing avoidable suffering. If the attraction is primarily the authority, the status or the deference attached to a designation, then I should be honest about that too.

Can I pursue achievement without making it the measure of my worth? Can I respect institutional authority without confusing it with moral superiority? Can I value financial security without looking down on people whose work is less rewarded?

These questions are uncomfortable precisely because they are not directed only at society. They are directed at me.

Beyond status: What should a good society measure?

Amartya Sen's capability approach offers a useful alternative to measuring social progress only through income or output. The central concern is what people are actually able to do and to be: whether they can be healthy, educated, safe, politically active, socially included and capable of pursuing lives they have reason to value.

This shifts the discussion. Instead of asking only how much an individual earns, we can ask whether they have access to healthcare, quality education, decent housing, meaningful work, leisure, security and the freedom to participate in society.

It also makes clear why the quality of public institutions matters. A society with good public healthcare, reliable transport, accessible education and effective social protection can expand individual freedom even when every person does not occupy a high-status profession.

None of this requires pretending that all work is identical. Some jobs require more training, carry greater responsibility or involve difficult conditions. Different levels of remuneration may be defensible for many reasons. But the dignity of the person should not be made conditional on the market price of their labour.

Perhaps a mature society is not one without distinctions of skill or responsibility. It is one that can recognise those distinctions without turning them into a ranking of human beings.

Questions I am still sitting with

Rather than pretending this inquiry has produced a neat conclusion, I want to leave a few questions open. They are not a test of ideological loyalty. They are invitations to examine assumptions we may carry without noticing.

If a profession lost its social prestige tomorrow but continued to provide the same income and satisfaction, would we still want to pursue it?

Do we admire people for what they contribute, or for the institutional symbols that surround them?

When we call someone successful, are we describing their capabilities, their income, their assets, their influence or the approval they receive from others?

Can a society claim to be meritocratic when the opportunity to develop and demonstrate merit is distributed unequally?

If a worker is essential to the functioning of a city but poorly paid, is the problem the worker's lack of skill, or the way society values and organises that work?

Does a secure salary make a person economically independent, or can it create a different form of dependence on institutions and employers?

If AI increases productivity while reducing the demand for some forms of labour, what institutions will ensure that purchasing power and economic security are not left behind?

Can democracy remain substantively equal when citizens have formally equal votes but radically unequal access to political influence?

How much of our ambition is driven by curiosity and the desire to contribute, and how much by the fear of being considered ordinary?

If our children chose occupations that were meaningful but socially unimpressive, would we support them as readily?

What would a society look like in which dignity was guaranteed, security was less precarious and status was not the primary language through which people recognised one another?

I do not think the answer is to reject ambition, markets, professional expertise or institutional authority. The task is to understand what each is good for, where each becomes distorted, and what rules are needed to prevent economic position from becoming a claim to greater human worth.

Perhaps the most difficult hierarchy to question is the one from which we ourselves hope to benefit.

And perhaps the real test is not whether we can criticise status from a distance, but whether we can recognise its influence in our own choices.

An unfinished inquiry

I am not arguing that every occupation should receive identical compensation, that expertise should not be recognised, or that ambition is inherently corrupt. I am asking whether the rewards attached to work have become confused with the worth of the people who perform it.

The hierarchy is not sustained by institutions alone. It is also sustained by ordinary acts of admiration, imitation, dismissal and comparison. We may criticise it in theory while reproducing it in our choices.

If our designations disappeared, our salaries became private information and nobody was impressed by our professional identities, what would remain of the way we understand ourselves?
