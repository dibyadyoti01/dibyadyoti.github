Reading list

Books, papers,
rabbit holes.

What is currently on my desk, in my Kindle, or somewhere inside an overactive brain.

Fiction

1. The Invention of Morel — Adolfo Bioy Casares

A tiny novel about an island, strange visitors and an impossible machine.

It reads almost like science fiction + metaphysics + Borges. Questions of reality, memory, immortality and perception emerge without the book becoming a philosophical treatise.

2. The Third Policeman — Flann O'Brien

Absolutely bizarre.

A narrator dies—or perhaps doesn't—and enters a world governed by absurd physics, bicycles, policemen and impossible logic.

It's one of those books where you eventually stop asking “What the hell is happening?” and start enjoying the fact that reality itself appears broken.

3. Solaris — Stanisław Lem

Forget the Hollywood version.

The real subject isn't aliens. It's the epistemological limits of human beings: what happens when humanity encounters something so fundamentally different that our scientific concepts cannot adequately describe it?

4. The Memory Police — Yoko Ogawa

On an island, objects gradually disappear from collective memory.

The interesting question isn't merely authoritarianism. It's:

What remains of a person when their memories, language and objects of reference disappear?

Very quiet, very strange.

5. The Unbearable Lightness of Being — Milan Kundera

Love, political history, bodily existence, chance and repetition.

The novel constantly moves between story and philosophical commentary, asking whether a life lived only once can have any objective meaning.

Economics

1. The Great Transformation — Karl Polanyi

One of the most important heterodox economics books.

Polanyi asks a profound question:

How did human societies transform economic activity from something embedded in social relationships into a relatively autonomous “market”?

Excellent for understanding capitalism, institutions, industrialisation and political economy.

2. The Logic of Collective Action — Mancur Olson

This is particularly useful for someone interested in governance.

Why don't groups always act in their collective interest?

Olson's answer leads into free-riding, public goods, interest groups and institutional design.

It's one of those books that changes how you look at bureaucracies and political organisations.

3. Debt: The First 5,000 Years — David Graeber

Anthropology meets economics.

Graeber challenges the conventional story that money naturally evolved from barter. His broader argument concerns debt, morality, power and social organisation.

Read it critically—Graeber is influential but controversial.

Physics

1. What Is Real? — Adam Becker

Probably my strongest recommendation for you.

It's about the foundations of quantum mechanics—not merely the equations, but the competing interpretations and philosophical disputes surrounding what quantum mechanics actually says about reality.

  • Copenhagen interpretation
  • Many-worlds
  • Hidden variables
  • Bell's theorem
  • Quantum foundations
  • Einstein vs Bohr

This is much closer to the question “What does quantum mechanics mean?” than a conventional QM textbook.

2. The Order of Time — Carlo Rovelli

A poetic but scientifically serious exploration of time.

The really interesting part is that the familiar concept of a universal flowing “present” doesn't survive modern physics in the way everyday intuition suggests.

Short and worth rereading.
